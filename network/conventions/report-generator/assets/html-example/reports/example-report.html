      Abstract

      Synthetic example

      This report shows the HTML template filled in end to end. Its data were generated for the example and describe no real experiment.

      We asked whether Drug X slows the growth of cultured lung cancer cells, compared with cells given only the drug's solvent (vehicle). Cells were counted daily for ten days at two doses. At the high dose the growth rate fell by 41.2%, and the effect grew steadily with dose. The treated cells stayed alive: 93% were viable at the end of the experiment, so the drug slows division rather than killing cells. All experiments used one cell line, so we do not yet know whether the effect generalizes.

      Problem statement

      A drug can stop a tumor from growing in two different ways: it can kill the cells, or it can slow how often they divide. The second kind of drug is called cytostatic (it slows or halts cell division without killing cells). The distinction matters because a cytostatic drug has to be combined with something else to shrink a tumor.

      This report answers one question: does Drug X slow the growth of lung cancer cells in culture, and if it does, is that because the cells die or because they divide less often? The comparison throughout is cells given the vehicle alone.

      Methods

      We grew A549 lung cancer cells in 4 replicate wells per condition and counted them once a day. Each condition received vehicle, a low dose (1 µM), or a high dose (5 µM) of Drug X. From each growth curve we estimated a doubling time: the time the population takes to double while it is still growing freely. A separate dose series measured growth rate at more doses so that we could fit a dose–response curve. Figure shows the design; the supplement gives the fitting details.

      Results

      Drug X slows growth, and higher doses slow it more

      Cells given vehicle doubled every 22.4 hours. The low dose stretched the doubling time to 27.9 hours and the high dose to 38.1 hours, which is a 41.2% reduction in growth rate at the high dose. Figure shows the daily counts; drag the slider to compare the conditions on any day.

      Growth rate follows a standard dose–response curve

      Across the dose series, growth rate fell along a sigmoid curve (Figure). Half of the maximal effect was reached at 1.8 µM, with a 95% bootstrap interval from 1.4 to 2.3 µM. This is a half-maximal dose for growth rate, not for cell death.

      Treated cells stay alive, so Drug X is cytostatic

      If the drug killed cells, the slower growth could simply reflect fewer living cells. It did not: at the high dose 93% of cells were alive at the end of the experiment. Drug X slows division rather than killing cells.

      Conclusions

      Drug X slows the growth of cultured lung cancer cells in proportion to dose, and it does so without killing them. On its own it would hold a tumor back rather than shrink it.

      Main caveat

      Every experiment used one cell line (A549). The effect may be specific to it.

      Next steps

      Repeat the growth assay across a panel of lung cancer cell lines, and test whether combining Drug X with a cytotoxic drug reduces cell number.
